Abstract The inter‐pore fluid exchange, driven by molecular diffusion, is referred to as pore coupling. The
pore coupling affects subsurface pore‐scale physicochemical dynamics. Monitoring pore coupling is essential
for understanding numerous hydrological and ecological processes. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) has
effectively tracked pore coupling in saturated porous media. However, monitoring unsaturated pore coupling
with NMR is challenging due to the complex pore network architecture and dynamic water‐pathway
connectivity. Based on X‐ray computed tomography imaging, we develop a framework to simulate the NMR
T
2
‐distribution and T
2
‐store‐ T
2
map of samples with distinct‐sized pore environments at different water
saturations, validated against measurements. NMR results, combined with visualized pore networks and phase
distributions, demonstrate that reduced saturation induces increasingly tortuous and eventually disconnected
water‐pathways, thus restricting and ultimately blocking pore coupling. Our work enhances NMR applications
in unsaturated media, providing accurate insights into the complex architecture and hydrodynamics in the pore
network.
Plain Language Summary Fluid is widely retained within the pore network in shallow vadose zone
soils, weathered bedrock, and deep oil and gas formations. Monitoring inter‐pore fluid diffusion and exchange
(pore coupling) through the pore network is crucial for managing groundwater resources, preserving
ecosystems, and mitigating environmental hazards. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a powerful
geophysical method to investigate pore‐scale fluid dynamics. Although NMR can provide detailed insights into
pore coupling within saturated soils and rocks, its application in unsaturated subsurface materials remains
challenging, even at the laboratory scale. To address this challenge, we developed a framework to integrate
measurements and simulations to track the pore coupling and its NMR response under the unsaturated state.
First, using X‐ray micro computed tomography, we visualize detailed pore network structures and phase
distributions to simulate NMR results validated against measurements, investigating how pore coupling affects
unsaturated NMR responses. The results also show that as saturation decreases, weakening pore coupling
tracked by NMR detects more tortuous and eventually disconnected water‐pathways, enabling the evaluation of
connectivity in unsaturated pore networks by applying NMR to monitor pore coupling. Our framework
enhances our understanding of how NMR can be applied in tracking hydrodynamics within a partially saturated
subsurface environment.
1. Introduction
Extensive pore networks exist throughout subsurface porous media, from shallow vadose zone soils to deep
aquifers and hydrocarbon reservoirs, governing underground fluid migration and storage. Within these pore
networks, fluid molecules continuously diffuse and exchange between pores (e.g., Anand & Hirasaki, 2007 ;
Grunewald & Knight, 2009 ; Toumelin et al., 2003 ), a process known as diffusional pore coupling. This inter‐pore
exchange influences pore‐environment behaviors, enhancing pore‐scale heat redistribution (Koch et al., 2021 ),
promoting solute mixing (e.g., Baqer & Chen, 2022 ; Sadeghi et al., 2003 ; Jiménez‐Martínez et al., 2020 ; Perez
et al., 2023 ), and affecting pore‐scale fluid‐rock interactions by modifying local concentration gradients.
Consequently, pore coupling alters key hydrodynamic and thermodynamic processes and regulates reaction ki-
netics within the subsurface. These effects influence natural processes such as freeze–thaw dynamics in
permafrost aggregates (Rooney et al., 2022 ) and contaminant migration in the vadose zone (Chen et al., 2023 ), as
well as engineered applications such as recovery and management of hydrocarbon reservoirs (Li et al., 2024 ).

Despite its importance, the mechanisms controlling pore coupling remain poorly understood. Previous experi-
mental studies have visualized diffusion‐driven transport and its effects on pore‐scale fluid kinetics in millifluidic
or microfluidic devices with simplified pore systems using imaging techniques such as scanning electrochemical
microscopy or high‐speed camera (e.g., Jiménez‐Martínez et al., 2015 ; Liu et al., 2016 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Zhou
et al., 2022 ). Numerical simulations have also been applied to model pore‐scale diffusion reactions based on
idealized pore network models (e.g., Collin et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2017 ). However, these idealized frameworks
lack the complexity of natural pore network architectures and cannot capture the dynamic connectivity of water‐
pathways in partially saturated materials, where solid–liquid–vapor interactions strongly influence molecular
diffusion. In particular, how the degree of pore coupling changes with decreasing saturation, and how this change
can be observed and quantified, remains unresolved.
Low‐field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) provides a powerful, non‐invasive way to investigate fluid
behavior within porous media due to its sensitivity to hydrogen in fluid molecules (Coates et al., 1999 ; Kirkland &
Codd, 2018 ; Prammer, 2004 ). NMR measures magnetization and relaxation times ( T
1
or T
2
) of protons after an
applied magnetic field removal to estimate water content, pore size distribution, and hydraulic conductivity
(Behroozmand et al., 2015 ). In multimodal pore systems, pore coupling drives inter‐pore exchange of magnetized
protons, as shown in Figures 1a and 1b . This exchange averages NMR relaxation behavior (Soto Bravo &
Zhang, 2023 ), causing T
2
peaks to shift or merge, which masks individual pore signatures and can make a
multimodal system appear unimodal (Ramakrishnan et al., 1999 ). The 2D T
2
‐store‐ T
2
map captures this process
through asymmetric off‐diagonal peaks, which indicate magnetized proton exchange between pores and reflect
both proton chemical shifts and inter‐pore molecular exchange during mixing periods (Song, 2013 ; F. Zhang &
Zhang, 2021 ). While NMR effectively tracks pore coupling in fully saturated materials (e.g., Anand & Hir-
asaki, 2007 ; Grunewald & Knight, 2009 ), variations in water‐pathway geometry and connectivity complicate its
application under unsaturated conditions. Accurately quantifying pore coupling and its NMR responses in un-
saturated porous media is crucial to fully leverage NMR's potential for investigating pore‐scale hydrodynamics
and advancing understanding of subsurface physicochemical processes such as mineral dissolution and soil
swelling.
To address this knowledge gap, we develop a framework combining NMR simulation and measurement with X‐
ray micro computed tomography (μCT) imaging to investigate pore coupling evolution in unsaturated
multimodal‐pore systems. Specifically, we: (a) integrate μCT imaging to visualize detailed pore network
structures and phase distributions; (b) simulate NMR T
2
‐distributions and T
2
‐store‐ T
2
map at varying saturations
and validate against laboratory measurements; (c) quantify how decreasing saturation weakens pore coupling by
tracking increasingly tortuous and eventually disconnected water pathways. Our results substantially enhance
NMR applications for characterizing unsaturated media and reveal how water‐pathway connectivity governs pore
coupling evolution, with implications for field‐scale NMR investigations of subsurface hydrodynamics.

2.3. μCT Imaging and NMR Responses Simulation
The μCT imaging of samples was conducted using the HECTOR system (Masschaele et al., 2013 ) at the Ghent
University Centre for X‐ray Tomography (UGCT). The μCT setup is shown in Figure 1d . To achieve high‐
resolution imaging of phase distributions, smaller sample tubes (5 mm inner diameter and 20 mm length) were
used compared to those for NMR measurements. Samples with different water saturations were prepared with
2.5% potassium iodide (KI) solution to enhance phase contrast. Sealed samples were scanned at 3 μm voxel
resolution, and three‐phase segmentation (solid, fluid, and air) was performed on reconstructed 2D grayscale
cross‐sections along the vertical height (Figure 1b ). These segmented images form a 3D volume visualizing pore
network structures and phase distribution in samples.
To reduce computational cost and uncertainty, pore network models were constructed directly from 2D phase‐
segmented images and used to simulate NMR responses via a commercial finite element software. Images of
each sample were clustered by vertical position, which is identified by similar phase distribution and pore ge-
ometry. Each cluster containing an equal number of images represents an equal‐length height range. One
representative image from each cluster was selected for pore network model construction. The fluid‐occupied
space from selected images was meshed, incorporating boundary conditions at fluid–air and fluid–solid in-
terfaces, to solve the Bloch–Torrey equation for time‐dependent magnetization distribution. Temporal variations
in the external magnetic field in the Bloch‐Torrey equation were accounted for to accurately simulate both CPMG
and TREx pulse sequences. Magnetization signals from ten representative simulations were superimposed and
inverted to T
2
‐distributions and 2D T

3. Results and Discussions
We combined μCT‐based NMR simulation with experimental validation to quantify how pore coupling evolves
with saturation. Both T
2
‐distribution and T
2
‐store‐ T
2
map reveal progressive weakening of pore coupling as
saturation decreases, corresponding to increasingly tortuous and eventually disconnected water‐pathways. This
framework provides quantitative metrics for tracking pore coupling in unsaturated multimodal‐pore networks.

The aforementioned evolution illustrates how saturation‐induced variations in water‐pathway geometry and inter‐

thereby restricting the inter‐pore molecular exchange. At low saturation, insufficient water fails to maintain inter‐
pore connectivity. The interconnected water‐occupied space progressively segregates into several smaller pore
clusters with limited connectivity. Eventually, these clusters fragments into isolated and individual pore envi-

is blocked, eliminating the pore coupling.
4. Geophysical Implications and Broader Applications
Our results demonstrate that decreasing water saturation leads to progressively weaker pore coupling due to
increased tortuosity and disconnection of water pathways. This process is directly reflected in the merging or
broadening of T
2
peaks and the loss of off‐diagonal features in T
2
–store– T
2
maps. This relationship has broad
implications for interpreting NMR data in hydrological models that can be used for groundwater management and
environmental remediation.
By quantifying coupling strength under variable saturation, NMR can inform parameterizations in dual‐porosity
or mobile‐immobile domain models, especially in predicting the retention, release, or accessibility of water and
solutes across pore scales. Furthermore, common NMR‐derived permeability estimation models such as the
Schlumberger Doll Research (Kenyon et al., 1988 ) and Timur‐Coates (Coates et al., 1999 ; Timur, 1969 ) models
are calibrated for saturated conditions and may significantly misestimate hydraulic conductivity in unsaturated
vadose zones. While several previous studies have applied NMR‐derived parameters to estimate subsurface
hydraulic conductivity in unsaturated media (e.g., Legchenko et al., 2004 ; Legchenko & Vouillamoz, 2011 ;
Vouillamoz et al., 2003 ), these approaches generally rely on averaged relaxation properties and do not explicitly
account for pore coupling, which reflects saturation‐dependent inter‐pore connectivity. As a result, permeability
estimates may be inaccurate under partially saturated conditions. The degree of pore coupling, quantified through
A
od
/ A
tot
and α
r
, provides correction factors for estimating hydraulic conductivity in partially saturated conditions.
Field NMR instruments, including surface NMR and borehole NMR, detect T
2
‐distribution profiles along depth
in the natural subsurface (e.g., Guérin, 2005 ; Jiang et al., 2020 ; Kirkland & Codd, 2018 ). Our findings clarify that
vertical variations in T
2
spectra might reflect pore coupling evolution due to saturation changes. This distinction
enhances field data interpretation, particularly in heterogeneous formations where saturation gradients are strong.
Beyond NMR, other geophysical methods such as spectral induced polarization (SIP) are sensitive to pore‐scale
connectivity and diffusion‐controlled charge transport (Tsukanov et al., 2021 ), the same processes quantified
through our pore coupling framework. Integrating NMR measurements with SIP and other geophysical methods
enables more robust characterization of subsurface fluid dynamics and reactive transport, improving predictive
models of water–rock interactions in complex geologic environments.
5. Conclusion
This study aims to clarify how NMR responds to the pore coupling in unsaturated porous media, and how
variation in saturation governs the pore coupling strength. Specifically, this paper develops a framework
combining simulation and measurement to investigate pore coupling and its NMR responses in unsaturated
porous media and provides insights into investigating hydrodynamics within complex subsurface geologic en-
vironments through NMR observation.
